Security Risk as a Dynamical System: Continuous Evidence, Measured Recovery, Real Leverage

Evidence reciprocity is an architecture decision. Authorization reciprocity is a government decision. Stop waiting for the second to do the first.

Federal compliance uses one word, reciprocity, for two different things. One is a government decision: an authorizing official accepts a risk determination, signs an authorization to operate, and other agencies rely on it instead of repeating the work. The other is an architecture decision: expressing a single control implementation, authored once, in the shape each framework asks for. The first is slow on purpose, and no amount of engineering speeds it up. The second can be built today, and most of the cost people blame on missing reciprocity is really the absence of the second.

This is a field report on building the second. The system is a real, running site whose every deploy derives a set of signed, machine-readable compliance documents from one source of truth, re-checks them against the live infrastructure, and publishes them where anyone can verify them with curl, jq, and cosign. It is a self-attested proof of concept (one operator, one cloud account, no federal data, no agency sponsor), so it demonstrates the mechanism, not an authorization. The mechanism is what generalizes; the single-site specifics are stand-ins.

Shifting reciprocity left

Compliance is normally a back-end activity. You build the system, then afterward you assemble a security plan that describes it, hand the package to an assessor, and wait. The plan is a snapshot the moment it is written and begins drifting from the running system immediately, and reconciling that drift is where most assessment effort, and most audit findings, go.

Shifting reciprocity left means moving evidence production into the pipeline that builds and runs the system, so the compliance state is a continuously maintained output rather than a periodic document. Concretely, that is five moves:

  • One canonical inventory. Every deploy inventories the live system, its resources and dependencies and build provenance, into a single machine-readable signal. That signal is the source of truth; nothing downstream is hand-maintained.
  • Derived, not authored, documents. The security plan, the plan of action and milestones, and the vulnerability report are generated from that inventory on every deploy. A document cannot drift from a system that regenerates it from the system's own state.
  • Authored once, projected many. The control evidence is written one time, against NIST 800-53, and projected into whatever shape a framework wants (FedRAMP, CMMC, a state program) through published crosswalks. Supporting another framework costs a mapping file, not another control-writing project. This is evidence reciprocity: the same facts, expressed in many vocabularies, composed rather than rewritten.
  • Fail closed, then sign. A reconciliation gate runs before anything is published and refuses the build unless every document carries the same inventory identity and this commit's hash, and unless the live account reconciles against the inventory. Only past that gate are artifacts signed and published.
  • Re-verified at runtime. A scheduled job re-evaluates the live configuration against the same policy the deploy gate used and re-signs the result every day, so the published state reflects the system as it is now, not as it was at the last code change.

The result is a compliance posture that is continuous, tamper-evident, and verifiable by a third party without taking the producer's word for it. Those are the properties reciprocity is meant to deliver, produced as a byproduct of shipping.

What continuity actually buys

Continuous compliance is usually sold on speed: the same documents, produced faster and more often. That undersells it, and it misses where the difficulty actually is. The claim underneath is a control claim. A compliance posture is a system that drifts, and the only thing that keeps drift bounded is a correction loop that closes faster than the drift accumulates. An annual assessment is not a slow version of that loop. It is an open loop with a report at the end.

Stated that way, the design follows. The daily runtime re-evaluation is not a nicety; it sets the loop's period, and the period decides how far the system can travel before anything notices. The reconciliation gate is not a formality; it is the comparator, the step that gets to say the live account and the documents disagree. Signing is what makes the loop's output usable by somebody who was not present when it ran. Each of the five moves above is a component of a controller, and the interesting property of a controller is not its throughput. It is how long an error can persist before it is corrected.

Which is where the honest limits are, and they are worth stating in the same vocabulary.

Cadence is not signal. A regime that mandates persistent validation gets you uniform sampling, and uniform sampling of the wrong quantity is still the wrong quantity. What this pipeline emits on a fixed schedule is a set of binary verdicts held at pass by a gate built to hold them there. That is the gate working as intended, and it is also what makes the series nearly informationless as a measurement: a control that is working suppresses the variation you would need in order to measure how well it is working.

The gate is a control input, not an instrument. Enforcing compliance is an intervention on the dynamics you would have to observe to know whether compliance is stable. There is no version of this architecture that reads the system from outside it, because the publishing step is inside the loop it reports on. That is not a defect to be engineered away; it is the standing condition of anything that measures itself, and the useful response is to say so rather than to present the output as a neutral reading. The longer argument is in Being in the Loop.

A false positive is a perturbation with no recovery loop attached. A finding that is wrong does not decay, because nothing is going to remediate it, and it does not diverge either. It sits at its initial value until somebody dispositions it. Findings held that way are the failure mode this architecture has to watch for most closely, because on a dashboard they are indistinguishable from a real problem nobody is fixing, and because an operator who learns to expect them stops reading the channel they arrive on. That is why dispositions carry a written rationale and are never silently dropped, and why the deploy-time and runtime verdicts are reconciled against each other rather than trusted separately.

What this pipeline can honestly demonstrate is the instrumentation: that a compliance program can be made to emit a continuous, signed, independently checkable record of its own state at all. It does not demonstrate that such a record is a sufficient basis for predicting failure, and nothing here should be read as claiming it does.

What the verdict covers

A daily verdict of converged, no regressions is only worth what it covers, so here is the coverage, and it is layered rather than uniform. The canonical inventory currently holds 214 components, checked by three mechanisms on three different cadences.

ComponentsMechanismCadence
140 dependenciesGrype and Dependabot against the dependency setevery build, plus a daily rebuild against a freshly pulled vulnerability database
43 content artifactssha256 reconciliation against the inventoryevery deploy
31 cloud resourcespolicy re-evaluation against the live accountdaily, and this is the layer with a gap

Of those 31 cloud resources, the daily runtime job re-validates 5 — a coverage of 5/31, spanning 3/15 of the resource types in the inventory. It re-checks object stores, the secret, and the distribution. It does not re-check the customer-managed keys, the roles and policies, the log groups, the certificate or the DNS zone. Those are evaluated by the deploy-time gate against the Terraform plan, which is a different claim: that the code describing them passed policy, not that the deployed resource still matches it today.

The gap is not an oversight to be apologized for, and it is not closed by writing more checks and declaring victory either. It is the quantity the outer loop exists to watch. The inner loop — deploy, gate, re-validate, reconcile — answers does the configuration match the policy, and it answers it well enough that the answer has been the same every day for months. The outer loop asks the harder question: does the policy still match the system. Those are different error signals, and only the second one moves.

It moves in a specific and unfriendly way. The inventory grows as dependencies and pages are added, and the runtime job re-validates the resource types it was written for. Nothing in the inner loop reports that ratio, because it is not an inner-loop question; a verdict that covers less of the system than it did last month is still a green verdict. That is the shape of dark debt in a compliance program, and the reason the figure above is generated from the inventory on every build rather than written down once. If it decays, it decays in public.

Two honest limits on reading it. The ratio is not a score to be maximized, because re-validating everything continuously would cost more than it returns and some resources genuinely do not drift. And the coverage figure says nothing about whether the checks themselves are the right ones — a system can re-validate every resource it owns against policies that have quietly stopped describing its risks. Measuring that is harder and this does not attempt it.

The system, and how it proves each control

The system is samaydlette.com: a static personal website on AWS that doubles as the continuous-compliance pipeline described here, plus a small authenticated web application behind it. The authorization boundary is a set of managed AWS services, deployed from GitHub Actions over OIDC: CloudFront and S3 for the site, API Gateway and Lambda for the application, Cognito for application sign-in, Route 53 (DNSSEC-signed) for DNS, ACM for TLS, KMS for keys, Secrets Manager for the one application secret, and CloudWatch for logs. Physical, environmental, and media-protection families are inherited from AWS's own FedRAMP authorization and cited, not re-implemented. The system is rated FIPS 199 Moderate / FedRAMP 20x Class C throughout.

What follows is how it meets each major group of control requirements, and where the machine-readable evidence for each lives. The grouping is by function rather than by family number, but every claim below maps to specific controls in the generated security plan.

Identity, authentication, and access (IA, AC). Application users sign in through Cognito with a password and a time-based one-time-password second factor; accounts are operator-created, not self-registered. The deploy pipeline authenticates to AWS through GitHub OIDC, a short-lived, workload-bound credential, so there is no long-lived access key to leak or rotate. Inside AWS, permissions follow least privilege: the deploy role carries only the actions it needs, policies attach to groups rather than users, and a separate read-only role exists for assessment access with an explicit deny on secret retrieval. The IAM policy bodies travel in the inventory as SHA-256 hashes, so a reviewer can confirm the deployed policy matches the documented one without the policy text being public.

Continuous monitoring and assessment (CA, RA, SI, AU, SR). This is the center of the system. A scheduled Lambda re-evaluates the live AWS configuration every day against the exact compiled policy the deploy gate runs, and re-signs the result, so drift between code changes surfaces within a day rather than at the next audit. Dependencies are scanned on every build (Grype and Dependabot) and the application is scanned dynamically (a monthly OWASP ZAP run); findings flow into a vulnerability report that scores each on the FedRAMP 20x PAIN scale, checks it against the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and blocks the deploy if anything exceeds the Class C remediation tolerance. Audit logs land in CloudWatch with a year's retention. The reconciliation gate is itself a continuous-assessment control: it fails the build when the documents and the live account disagree. Supply-chain integrity is covered by a software bill of materials and the signing chain below.

Cryptographic protection (SC). Everything in transit is TLS: CloudFront redirects to HTTPS and negotiates TLS 1.2 or better with an ACM-issued certificate, and origin connections are HTTPS-only. Everything at rest is encrypted; buckets and log groups use SSE-KMS under four customer-managed keys, one each for data at rest, the DNSSEC key-signing key, the runtime signing key, and the application secret. DNS is DNSSEC-signed. The compliance artifacts themselves are signed two ways: deploy-time documents with Sigstore keyless signing (a short-lived certificate bound to the GitHub workflow identity and recorded in the public Rekor transparency log, with no private key to custody), and the daily runtime signal with an ECDSA key in KMS whose public half is published for anyone to verify against.

Configuration and change management (CM, SA). The entire system is defined in Terraform; there is no console-clicked infrastructure. Every change takes one path: a pull request, an Open Policy Agent evaluation of the Terraform plan and the rendered site that blocks on any violation, then an apply. Each commit is tagged with a change-significance type, and the git history is the change record. Because the security plan, the POA&M, and the vulnerability report are generated from the inventory on every deploy, they cannot describe a configuration the system no longer has. The usual gap between a security plan and the live system is closed automatically. Infrastructure-as-code scanners (Checkov, tfsec) run in the same pipeline, and any finding is either fixed or recorded as a dispositioned, risk-accepted item with a written rationale, never silently dropped.

Incident response and contingency (IR, CP). Detection is the daily drift check and the build-time gates; a documented incident-response plan covers the human side. Recovery leans on the same property that makes the documents trustworthy: the whole system is reproducible from source. The site content, the infrastructure, and the compliance pipeline all live in one Git repository, so rebuilding is a terraform apply rather than a restore-from-backup exercise, and S3 versioning protects the served content. The compliance Lambda has a dead-letter queue so a failed run is captured rather than lost. The single-region footprint is a deliberate, documented residual for a system at this impact level, not an oversight.

Boundary protection (SC-7, SC-5). Nothing is exposed to the internet directly; all traffic arrives through CloudFront and API Gateway, both managed interfaces, with AWS Shield Standard and request throttling in front. A web application firewall is intentionally declined, because the attack surface is a static site plus a single throttled, authenticated API; its incremental layer-7 filtering is documented as a residual rather than pretended away.

One implementation, many frameworks. All of the above is written against NIST 800-53 Rev 5, once. From that single set of control implementations (115 hand-written, the rest resolved by family-level defaults, 333 in all), the pipeline projects a security plan for each framework a reader might ask about: FedRAMP Rev 5 Moderate (333/333 requirements addressed), FedRAMP 20x Class C, NIST 800-53 Rev 4, CMMC Level 2 (110/110), GovRAMP Moderate with the CJIS overlay (339/339), and TX-RAMP Levels 1 (122/122) and 2 (322/322). Each is a profile or a crosswalk plus one projection run; none required rewriting a control. A consuming organization gets the same treatment in reverse: a single check of its own configuration reports every framework requirement that check satisfies at once. The package is built to conform to FedRAMP's Consolidated Rules for 2026, mechanized ahead of the deadline rather than after it.

Measuring the loop

If the worth of a compliance loop is set by how fast it closes, then the quantity to measure is recovery: how long a disturbance persists before the system is back where it belongs. That is measurable from telemetry a compliance program already emits. It is also the place where the obvious measurement is the wrong one, so this section is what the measurement takes, what to avoid, and what it would look like for the claim to fail. The companion essay makes the case for measuring this at all, in words and without the algebra; the formal treatment is here.

The number being measured

The companion essay argues for this quantity in words and keeps the equations out on purpose. They belong here. What follows is the whole of the formal apparatus the rest of this section runs on, and every line of it carries a plain-language reading, because a formula nobody can state in a sentence is a formula nobody should act on.

The convention, fixed once. Write the state as a displacement $\delta$ from the equilibrium the system rests at. Near that point the motion is $\dot{\delta} = -H\delta$, where $H$ is the stiffness matrix, measuring how steeply the basin walls rise in each direction. Its eigenvalues $0 < \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots$ are return rates, and the smallest of them, $\lambda_1$, is the softest direction: the one that gives way first. Much of the literature works instead with the Jacobian $J = -H$, whose leading eigenvalue is $-\lambda_1$, and every sign below inverts under that reading. I count restoring stiffness so the number falls as the system gets worse, which is the direction a risk audience reads without having to translate. In plain language: $\lambda_1$ is how hard the system pulls itself back along its weakest direction. Zero means it no longer does.

Critical slowing down.

$$\tau = \frac{1}{\lambda_1} \to \infty \quad \text{as} \quad \lambda_1 \to 0^+$$

Recovery time is one over the return rate. As the restoring pull vanishes, the time to come back from a disturbance grows without bound. That divergence is what the whole measurement programme is chasing.

Observable signatures. Drive that linear system with white noise of variance $\sigma^2_{\text{forcing}}$ and it becomes an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, whose stationary statistics along the softest direction are

$$\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{forcing}}}{2\lambda_1} \qquad \rho(\Delta t) = e^{-\lambda_1 \Delta t} \to 1 \ \text{ as } \ \lambda_1 \to 0^+$$

Variance rises because disturbances persist longer and pile up on each other. Lag-1 autocorrelation climbs toward one because a system recovering ever more slowly increasingly just repeats where it already was. Both are readable from a time series with no knowledge of the equations underneath, which is what makes them useful and also what makes them easy to over-read: they have one common cause, so observing both is a single measurement and not two.

The square root, and why it is bad news. The canonical saddle-node is $dx/dt = \mu - x^2$, with $\mu$ the distance to the fold. Its equilibria sit at $x^* = \pm\sqrt{\mu}$, and on the stable branch the return rate is

$$\lambda_1 = 2\sqrt{\mu} \ \longrightarrow \ 0 \quad \text{as} \quad \mu \to 0^+$$

so recovery time diverges as $\mu^{-1/2}$. The return rate falls to zero as the square root of the distance to the tipping point, which is an unfriendly shape for a warning system. The signal is flat and small across most of the approach and then moves fast at the very end, so it is weakest over the interval where there would still be time to act on it. Every early-warning scheme built on this inherits that shape, and no amount of estimator work removes it.

What none of it covers. Every expression above assumes $H$ holds still over the estimation window. The essay is about why that assumption fails hardest in systems that reorganize when they sense their own boundary, which is the regime these methods exist to detect.

The system being modeled

The arguments below come from dynamical systems, and they apply to a system only once it has been defined. The object of study is a staffed security operation: a team with finite attention working a queue of vulnerabilities that arrives whether or not they are keeping up. Not this website, which is the special case at the end of this section.

Two state variables carry the behavior. $V$ is the count of open vulnerabilities. $R$ is effective response capacity, in full-time equivalents. Capacity is a state variable, not a parameter: it is acted on by the queue instead of sitting outside it. That is the whole difference between this and a conventional risk model, and everything interesting follows from it.

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \alpha - \frac{\beta R V}{V + K_v} - \eta V \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma(R_0 - R) - \delta V$$

Reading the terms in order. $\alpha$ is the rate at which vulnerabilities arrive, and it is the parameter the system is pushed on. $\beta R V/(V + K_v)$ is remediation, in Michaelis–Menten form: linear in $V$ when the queue is short, saturating at $\beta R$ when it is long, with $K_v$ the half-saturation constant. That saturation is the empirical claim: a team clearing a short queue goes faster per item than the same team clearing a long one. $\eta V$ is natural decay, as the environment rotates and upstream vendors patch. $\gamma(R_0 - R)$ is capacity recovering toward its baseline $R_0$. And $\delta V$ is capacity drain: every open vulnerability consumes some attention, so a growing queue directly reduces the capacity available to shrink it.

That last term is where the failure comes from. It is a positive feedback loop between workload and incapacity, and it is why the failure is a cliff, not a slope.

Why this form and not the obvious one. The classical predator–prey model is the reflex choice for two coupled populations. It is wrong here twice over. Its coexistence equilibrium has $\det(J) = 0$ identically, which produces a center rather than a stable node or spiral: marginal stability as a structural artifact of the model rather than as a finding about the system. And it has no saddle-node, so under stress the stable state shifts instead of ceasing to exist. Saturating remediation and linear capacity drain fix both.

Stability. Setting $dR/dt = 0$ gives $R^* = R_0 - \delta V^*/\gamma$, and substituting into $dV/dt = 0$ leaves an equation nonlinear in $V^*$ with no closed form. Writing $J_{11} = -\beta R^* K_v/(V^* + K_v)^2 - \eta$ and $J_{12} = -\beta V^*/(V^* + K_v)$, the Jacobian is

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} J_{11} & J_{12} \\ -\delta & -\gamma \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{tr}(J) = J_{11} - \gamma < 0 \qquad \det(J) = \gamma\left(\frac{\beta R^* K_v}{(V^* + K_v)^2} + \eta\right) - \frac{\delta \beta V^*}{V^* + K_v}$$

with eigenvalues $\lambda_{1,2} = \left(\text{tr}(J) \pm \sqrt{\text{tr}(J)^2 - 4\det(J)}\right)/2$. The trace is negative wherever $R^* > 0$. The determinant is positive on the stable branch and falls to zero at the fold. That is the contrast with the predator–prey form, where a vanishing determinant is a standing property rather than a bifurcation being approached.

The cliff. As $\alpha$ rises toward a critical value $\alpha_c$, the stable node and a saddle collide and annihilate; at the collision $\det(J) = 0$ and $\lambda_1 = 0$. There is no closed form for $\alpha_c$, but it is located numerically as the arrival rate at which $\max(\text{Re}(\lambda_i)) = 0$. With $\beta = 0.5$, $K_v = 80$, $\eta = 0.01$, $\gamma = 0.2$, $R_0 = 15$ and $\delta = 0.005$, that is $\alpha_c \approx 6.09$ vulnerabilities per day. Below it a stable equilibrium exists and perturbations decay. Above it there is no equilibrium at all: vulnerabilities accumulate, capacity drains, and the coupling between them accelerates the divergence. The interesting operational fact is that the team at $\alpha = 5.5$ and the team at $\alpha = 6.5$ look almost identical on any static dashboard, and are in completely different dynamical regimes.

Status of that number. It is a property of a two-equation toy at parameters chosen to be plausible rather than measured. It is not a threshold anyone should adopt. What transfers is the shape: saturating response plus capacity drain gives a saddle-node, and a saddle-node gives a cliff with critical slowing down in front of it. The reason the shape transfers and the number does not is the deflationary theorem in the companion essay.

This site is not that system. samaydlette.com is operated by one person, so there is no staffing dynamic and no meaningful capacity-drain term. $\delta V$ is where the saddle-node comes from, so the model above describes an operation this site does not have. That is consistent with what its own telemetry shows: the adherence loop reads pass every day with no variance to fit anything to, because a control that is working suppresses the variation you would need in order to measure it. So the loop worth instrumenting here is the one in What the verdict covers, adequacy instead of adherence, and the estimators below are method rather than results.

Why not mean time to recover

One caution before any of it, because it invalidates a good deal of standard practice and what follows is a special case: heavy-tailed operational data breaks mean-based estimators, and most operational data is heavy-tailed. Any time anyone proposes a metric of the form "average time to X", ask first what the distribution looks like. If it has a long right tail, and incident data reliably does, the average is a number about the outliers rather than about the system.

The obvious operational proxy for $1/|\lambda_1|$ is mean time to recover. Proposition 1 in the essay says recovery time is the quantity that diverges at threshold, and everyone already tracks MTTR. So $\text{MTTR} \propto 1/|\lambda_1|$ is the obvious headline measurement to reach for.

It does not work. This is the error most likely to be caught immediately by the readers I most want, because the people who run incident response already know the counter-evidence.

The distribution is the problem. The Verica Open Incident Database has collected roughly 2,000 incident reports from around 660 organizations. The finding that matters here is about shape, not size. Incident duration data is strongly right-skewed with a long tail. Measures of central tendency do not represent it. The mean is dragged around by a handful of outliers, so the "average" describes the worst few incidents and says nothing about the typical one.

Two independent analyses take it further. Štěpán Davidovič ran Monte Carlo simulations and concluded that reliably calculating whether incident duration has improved is not possible using MTTR. The noise swamps the signal at realistic incident volumes. Lorin Hochstein has shown on Surfing Complexity that when durations follow a power-law distribution, an observed trend in MTTR conveys no useful information at all. The statistic wanders whether or not anything has changed.

And there is an internal contradiction. Power-law event distributions are routinely listed as a signature of criticality. The VOID data shows heavy tails in ordinary operations, everywhere, all the time, in organizations that are plainly not on the edge of collapse. So either every software organization is permanently at criticality, which makes the signature unfalsifiable and therefore worthless, or the heavy tail comes from somewhere else entirely. Heterogeneous incident classes lumped into one distribution. Detection thresholds truncating the left side. Reporting artifacts. The simple fact that "incident" names a category, not a phenomenon.

The second reading is obviously correct. It is why Proposition 2 there does not list power laws. A heavy tail in incident data is not evidence of criticality. It is evidence that unlike things have been pooled.

What to use instead. Two replacements, in order of preference.

  1. Perturbation-response fits on individually identified events: the primary recommendation. Identify a specific perturbation (a major CVE disclosure, a deployment, an analyst departure, a tool outage), then fit the exponential recovery of the deviation from baseline. The slope is $\lambda_1$, measured directly, not inferred from a summary statistic. This works because it never pools across incident classes: each event is its own measurement, with its own magnitude and its own recovery curve. It is more work than reading a dashboard number. It is also the statistically sound method.

  2. The distribution of recovery times, tracked by quantile: the fallback when you cannot identify discrete perturbations. Track the median and the 90th percentile separately and watch them move. A rising median with a stable p90 means something different from a stable median with a rising p90, and the mean collapses both into one uninformative number.

The approach to avoid is the aggregate-metric one: composite stability indices built from ratios of averages, say a product of remediation-to-introduction, detection, and capacity ratios. It is intuitive, it is easy to put on a slide, and it inherits every distributional problem above from each of its three factors at once.

Measuring recovery directly

This method exploits natural experiments: known perturbations followed by measurable recovery. Suitable events include a major CVE disclosure affecting the environment, a significant deployment or infrastructure change, an analyst departure or addition, a tool outage and restoration, or an audit finding requiring remediation.

Step 1. At time $t_0$, identify a perturbation $\delta\mathbf{K}$: a vulnerability spike, a queue surge, with a known magnitude.

Step 2. Track the deviation from the pre-perturbation baseline: $\Delta K_i(t) = K_i(t) - K_i^{\text{baseline}}$.

Step 3. For a stable system the deviation decays exponentially, $\Delta K_i(t) \approx \Delta K_i(t_0) e^{\lambda_1 (t - t_0)}$. Fit by log-linear regression:

$$\ln|\Delta K_i(t)| = \ln|\Delta K_i(t_0)| + \lambda_1 (t - t_0)$$

The slope is $\lambda_1$ directly.

Step 4. If multiple components are perturbed, the full recovery decomposes over modes, $\Delta\mathbf{K}(t) = \sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i e^{\lambda_i (t - t_0)}$, and principal component analysis of the recovery trajectory separates them.

Why this one is preferred: it never pools across incident classes. Each event carries its own magnitude and its own recovery curve, so the heavy-tail problem that destroys mean-based metrics does not arise. Report the resulting $\lambda_1$ estimates as a distribution across events, by quantile, not as an average.

The approach to avoid is the composite index, a stability score built from ratios of averages. It is intuitive, it fits on a slide, and it inherits every distributional problem above from each of its factors at once.

Reading the number

Eigenvalue Condition Operational Meaning Recommended Action
All $\text{Re}(\lambda_i) < -0.1$ Strong stability margin Monitor; maintain current processes
Dominant $-0.1 < \text{Re}(\lambda_1) < 0$ Reduced stability margin Increase monitoring frequency; review capacity
$\text{Re}(\lambda_1) \approx 0$ Threshold proximity Immediate intervention; add capacity; reduce load
$\text{Re}(\lambda_1) > 0$ Unstable; diverging Emergency response; stop non-critical changes; surge capacity

The numerical boundaries above are placeholders pending calibration against a specific environment. They are not thresholds anyone should adopt as given; $\lambda_1$ has units of inverse time, so the meaning of $-0.1$ depends entirely on the sampling interval and the domain's natural timescale.

Back-testing. Identify historical threshold crossings (major incidents, prolonged degradation), compute eigenvalue estimates for the periods leading up to them, check whether $\lambda_1 \to 0$ actually preceded the crossing, and calibrate warning thresholds against the lead time you need. Test robustness against different window sizes, first- versus second-order models, missing data, and outliers.

Ground truth validation. Where possible, compare eigenvalue-based predictions to actual incident rates, audit findings, red team/penetration test results, and known staffing or tooling degradation periods.

And the limit on all of it. Every method here estimates a Jacobian from history and assumes it still applies. The essay explains why that assumption fails hardest in the near-threshold regime these methods exist to detect. Back-testing partially conceals this problem instead of solving it: a historical crossing is an episode in which the organization was reorganizing, so a model fitted across it is fitted across several different vector fields. Good back-test performance is weaker evidence than it appears.

A prediction, stated in advance

A method that only explains what already happened is not worth much. So here is a bet, in a form that can come back false.

The prediction. In a security operations organization drifting toward a capacity threshold, the upper quantiles of the perturbation-recovery-time distribution will broaden before the incident rate rises, leading it by weeks, not following it. Concretely: track the ratio of the 90th percentile to the median recovery time across individually identified perturbations. That ratio should rise measurably while the incident count is still flat, and it should rise before any of the conventional indicators (queue depth, alert volume, MTTR itself) move.

Why this counts as novel. It was not the reason I built the framework. I came to this from $\lambda_1 \to 0$ in ecological models, where the standard prediction concerns variance and autocorrelation of a state variable. Mine concerns the shape of a recovery-time distribution. The distributional version fell out of abandoning MTTR, and the prediction falls out of taking the heavy tails seriously. It does not accommodate them. It also runs against the ordinary operational reading, which treats worsening recovery as a lagging consequence of a bad period rather than a warning of one.

How it fails. If the quantile ratio moves at the same time as the incident rate, it is a coincident indicator and useless for warning. If it moves afterwards, the causal story is backwards. If it moves at random with respect to incidents, the framework has no operational content in this domain and I should say so. And if the ratio is too noisy to estimate at realistic perturbation counts, the prediction is untestable as stated and needs a better estimator before it means anything. That last one is the Davidovič objection applied to my own replacement metric. It is a live possibility I cannot rule out.

That last failure mode is the one I consider most likely. I am stating the prediction anyway, because a method that will not name a way to lose cannot be checked.

Why this matters beyond the audit

The reason to build this is not to pass an audit faster, though it does that. It is that a compliance state produced this way is trustworthy in a way a document is not. Anyone can pull the signed artifacts from a URL and verify, independently, that they were produced by the named pipeline, that they match the live system, and that they are current as of today. Trust stops being a matter of believing the producer and becomes a matter of checking math. The rendered form of all of it is the live trust dashboard, which reads the same published artifacts this page describes. That check is one command: scripts/verify-published.sh in the public repository fetches the live artifacts, verifies their signatures against the pinned workflow identity, and reconciles them — from the public internet, with no credentials.

That property is exactly what two adjacent markets are missing. Cyber-insurance underwriting and third-party risk management both run on stale questionnaires and point-in-time attestations, because current, trustworthy, machine-queryable data about a system's actual posture has not existed. A continuously published, signed inventory and vulnerability report is that data. Whether underwriters and risk teams consume it is a question of adoption, not of engineering, since the feed is already there.

And the cost is the headline. The whole pipeline runs for a few dollars a month. Most of what gets blamed on a lack of reciprocity is not the government's slow, deliberate authorization decision, which should stay slow and deliberate. It is the absence of the evidence layer underneath it, the part any team can build without anyone's permission. This builds it. The authorization reciprocity stays where it belongs; the evidence reciprocity is available now.
